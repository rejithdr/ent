Snoring and Sleep Apnoea Treatment in Thrissur, for Children and Adults

Snoring is common, but it isn't always harmless. Sometimes it's a sign that breathing is being blocked during sleep. This page explains what to look for, when to see a doctor, and how it can be treated.

What is snoring?

Snoring is the sound made when the airway in the nose and throat partly narrows during sleep, and the soft tissues vibrate as air passes through.

Snoring sits on a range:

  • Simple snoring: noise only, with no breathing pauses and no effect on health. Treatment is optional.
  • Upper airway resistance: breathing takes more effort and sleep is disturbed, even without full pauses. Worth treating before it progresses.
  • Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA): breathing repeatedly stops or becomes very shallow for 10 seconds or more during sleep. This needs treatment. More about sleep apnoea.

Snoring in children

Children should not snore regularly. Regular snoring, especially with mouth breathing, usually means the airway is narrowed, most often by enlarged adenoids or tonsils.

Signs parents often miss

  • Snoring most nights
  • Sleeping with the mouth open, or breathing through the mouth during the day
  • Restless sleep, unusual sleeping positions, or frequent waking
  • Pauses in breathing, gasping or choking during sleep
  • Bedwetting after the child had stopped
  • Tiredness, irritability, hyperactivity or poor attention at school
  • Poor weight gain or slow growth

Why it matters

Long-term mouth breathing and poor sleep can affect a child's growth, behaviour, learning, and the development of the face and teeth. Early assessment gives the best chance to correct it.

Seek urgent medical care if your child has long pauses in breathing, turns blue or pale during sleep, or is struggling hard to breathe.

Recording a sleep video at home

A short video helps us see how your child breathes during sleep. Please record it like this:

  • Record during deep sleep, about 1–2 hours after your child falls asleep, or in the early morning.
  • If possible, your child should be lying on their back.
  • Show the chest, at least up to the collarbones, and ideally the whole chest, so we can see the breathing effort.
  • Make sure there is enough light to see breathing clearly.
  • Record for 2–3 minutes, including any snoring, pauses or struggling.
Your child's privacy matters to us. We never ask you to send your child's video by WhatsApp or any other app. Keep it on your phone and show it to us during the consultation.

How we treat children

Many children improve with medical treatment, such as nasal sprays and treatment of allergy, especially when symptoms are mild. We usually try this first and review progress.

When surgery is needed, we follow a defined, graded protocol. The amount of adenoid and tonsil tissue removed is planned according to your child's age and the problem being treated. Put simply, we remove only what is needed, and protect what works.

  • Endoscopic coblation adenoidectomy, with the surgeon viewing the area directly
  • Intracapsular tonsil surgery when appropriate, which usually means less pain and faster recovery
  • Most children go home the same day

If mouth breathing has affected the teeth or jaw, we may also suggest an orthodontic assessment.

Snoring and sleep apnoea in adults

In adults, loud snoring can be a sign of obstructive sleep apnoea. Many people with sleep apnoea don't know they have it, because it happens while they're asleep.

Warning signs

  • Loud snoring, often noticed by a partner
  • Someone has seen you stop breathing, gasp or choke during sleep
  • Feeling tired or falling asleep during the day, even after a full night in bed
  • Morning headaches, a dry mouth, or poor concentration
  • High blood pressure
  • Being overweight, or having a thick neck
Drowsy while driving? If you feel sleepy at the wheel, please don't drive until you have been assessed. Sleep apnoea is a recognised cause of road accidents.

Why it matters

Untreated sleep apnoea is linked with high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, diabetes, low mood and poor memory. Treatment can improve sleep, energy and long-term health.

How sleep apnoea is assessed

  • Consultation and examination of the nose, throat and airway
  • Sleep study to confirm the diagnosis and measure severity
  • Sleep endoscopy (DISE) in selected patients, to see exactly where the airway collapses during sleep before planning surgery

Treatment options

There is no single treatment for everyone. The right choice depends on how severe the sleep apnoea is and where the airway is blocked.

Lifestyle changes, including weight loss and avoiding alcohol at nightEveryone, as part of any treatment plan

For doctors: referring a patient

We welcome referrals from paediatricians, pulmonologists, physicians, dentists and orthodontists.

Consider referring

  • Children with habitual snoring, mouth breathing, witnessed apnoeas, enuresis, behavioural or growth concerns
  • Adults with suspected or confirmed OSA who are CPAP-intolerant, have nasal obstruction affecting CPAP use, or are being considered for surgery
  • Patients needing sleep endoscopy (DISE) for treatment planning
We keep you informed. After every referral, we send you a short update on our assessment and plan, and the patient returns to you for ongoing care.
